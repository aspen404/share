A little grown-up paperwork — and why it's actually kind of nice

Hi Kit,

Now that you're 18, you're officially your own adult — which is great. One small side effect: there's a tiny bit of standard paperwork that pretty much every adult sets up at some point. It's boring admin, not a big deal, and we'd like a lawyer to handle the actual work so you barely have to think about it.

There are three documents. Here's the whole point of all three in one sentence:

They let you choose who's allowed to help you with official stuff — so the people you trust can keep being there for you, exactly like always.

That's it. Before 18, parents could automatically step in on certain things (talk to a doctor's office, sort out a bill). After 18, the law doesn't assume that anymore. These documents just let you say, on your own terms, "yep, this person can help me." You stay in charge of everything.

You can change any of this whenever you want, for any reason. Nothing here is permanent. And you can fill this out a bit at a time — it saves as you go.

The three documents

1. A "who can help with health stuff" document

the lawyer may call it a health care power of attorney

This names one person you'd trust to talk to doctors on your behalf — but only if you ever couldn't do it yourself (say you were under anesthesia, or really sick for a few days). The rest of the time it does nothing at all. It's basically a backup so someone you trust can speak up for you instead of a stranger having to guess.

Your one decision — who's your person?

2. A "who can help with money / paperwork stuff" document

the lawyer may call it a financial power of attorney

Same idea, for practical things — like if you were traveling, or in the hospital, and a bill needed paying or a form needed signing and you couldn't get to it. It lets someone you trust handle that for you. Again, only if needed.

Your one decision — who's your person? (Most people pick the same as above.)

3. A simple will

short and simple at 18 — totally fine to keep it basic

A will just says who'd sort things out and where your belongings would go, in the very unlikely event something happened to you. It's completely okay to say "keep it simple, the usual" — the lawyer has a sensible default.
